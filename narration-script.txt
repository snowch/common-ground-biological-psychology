c01s03 Chapter 1: Biological psychology connects levels.
Biological psychology: study of biological contributions to behaviour. Neural correlate: brain measure associated with a process. Mechanism: a process proposed to produce an effect.

Behaviour involves interacting biological, psychological and social processes. A biological description adds a level of explanation; it does not remove the need to understand the task.

An association between a brain signal and an event is a starting point. It does not uniquely identify what one person thought.

c01s03b Chapter 1: Methods reveal different things.
Electroencephalography: recording electrical activity at the scalp. Functional imaging: estimating activity-related physiological changes. Lesion evidence: studying consequences of tissue damage.

Scalp electrical recordings have useful temporal resolution but limited source localization. Functional magnetic resonance imaging measures blood-oxygen-related changes rather than thoughts directly.

Lesions and carefully designed interventions can add causal evidence, but damage or stimulation can affect connected systems. Each method has practical and inferential limits.

c01s04 Chapter 1: Do not read a mind from a hotspot.
Reverse inference: inferring a process from associated brain activity. Converging evidence: support across suitable methods.

If fear sometimes involves a region, activity there does not prove fear. The same region may participate in attention, learning and other tasks.

A stronger account uses comparison conditions, task behaviour and information about specificity. Neural evidence should narrow alternatives rather than decorate a preferred story.

c01s05 Chapter 1: What does the evidence allow?
Functional imaging findings depend on comparisons, analysis choices and samples. Reverse inference is stronger when the observed pattern is selective and the prior alternatives are considered.

The café image is fictional. It supplies no diagnostic or individual mental-state evidence.

c02s03 Chapter 2: Cells maintain an electrical difference.
Ion channel: a membrane pathway for specific ions.

Neurons maintain differences in charged particles across a membrane. Inputs can change the membrane potential. Dendrites and the cell body receive many inputs, while an axon can carry a propagated signal.

These are useful functional distinctions, with variation across neuron types. A single neuron is not a miniature thinking person.

c02s03b Chapter 2: An action potential regenerates.
Action potential: a brief propagated membrane-voltage change. Refractory period: interval limiting immediate refiring.

When conditions reach threshold, voltage-sensitive channels support a rapid change in membrane potential. The process regenerates along the axon.

Individual spikes are broadly all-or-none. Information can depend on timing, firing rates and population patterns rather than a bigger spike meaning a stronger thought.

c02s04 Chapter 2: The connection changes the next cell.
Synapse: a junction for signalling between cells. Neurotransmitter: chemical messenger released at a synapse. Receptor: molecular target responsive to a messenger.

At many synapses, an arriving signal triggers chemical release. A transmitter binds receptors that affect the receiving cell. Other synapses transmit electrically.

The effect depends on receptors, cell state and circuitry. A transmitter is not inherently a single emotion, and more of it does not imply more happiness.

c02s05 Chapter 2: What does the evidence allow?
Cellular physiology supports detailed mechanisms of signalling, while links from those mechanisms to complex behaviour require additional levels of evidence.

Electrical and chemical descriptions are complementary. Neither identifies a personality trait or emotion from one cell.

c03s03 Chapter 3: Anatomy gives landmarks, not isolated minds.
Cerebral cortex: outer neural tissue of the cerebrum. Subcortical structure: structure beneath the cortex. Network: interacting set of connected regions.

Brain regions have different connections and contributions. Cortical and subcortical systems interact in perception, memory and action.

A task can involve several networks, and a region can contribute to several tasks. A labelled anatomical map is a starting point for understanding these interactions.

c03s03b Chapter 3: Lateralization is not a personality test.
Lateralization: unequal contribution across hemispheres. Corpus callosum: major connection between the hemispheres. Functional specialization: relative contribution to a function.

Some functions show hemispheric asymmetries. Many aspects of language are often left-lateralized, but variation and bilateral contributions matter.

Both hemispheres participate in complex reasoning and creative activity. Classifying a person as left-brained or right-brained is not supported by this anatomical distinction.

c03s04 Chapter 3: Damage affects connected systems.
Plasticity: experience-related change in a biological system. Distributed processing: a function depends on interacting components. Compensation: another route helps maintain performance.

A lesion can disrupt local tissue and its connections. Changes in performance help investigate a system, but task demands and the extent of damage matter.

Recovery can involve practice, support and reorganization. It is not evidence that all regions are interchangeable or that every injury has the same prognosis.

c03s05 Chapter 3: What does the evidence allow?
Neuroscience combines anatomy, task measures and interventions to examine functional organization. Region labels alone cannot establish a psychological explanation.

The café repair is not a localization experiment. Brain-based personality quizzes need independent validation rather than anatomical metaphors.

c04s03 Chapter 4: Energy must become a neural signal.
Sensory transduction: conversion of stimulation into cellular signalling. Sensory receptor: cell or ending responsive to stimulation.

Sound pressure, light and other physical events interact with specialized receptors. Transduction turns these interactions into biological signals.

Detection depends on the stimulus, receptor system and conditions. The physical presence of a sound does not guarantee that a listener received a usable message.

c04s03b Chapter 4: Sensory systems adapt.
Sensory adaptation: changed responsiveness during sustained stimulation. Absolute threshold: stimulus level detected under defined conditions. Difference threshold: change detectable under defined conditions.

Responsiveness can change with ongoing stimulation. Detection thresholds are measured probabilistically under specified conditions, not as an unchanging line for every person.

Noise, fatigue, attention and sensory access affect performance. Compare the actual listening conditions before assigning a psychological motive to a missed message.

c04s04 Chapter 4: Perception uses more than receptors.
Perception: organization and interpretation of sensory information. Multisensory integration: combining information across senses. Top-down influence: effects of knowledge or expectation on processing.

A visible speaker, clear context or written message can improve interpretation. Expectations can also favour a mistaken reading of an ambiguous signal.

Biological pathways and cognitive interpretation work together. Provide accessible information and check understanding instead of assuming that a loud enough signal solves every difficulty.

c04s05 Chapter 4: What does the evidence allow?
Sensory physiology identifies transduction and pathways; perceptual research investigates how information is selected and interpreted. A demonstration does not diagnose an individual sensory condition.

Thresholds depend on measurement procedures and response criteria. Persistent sensory concerns warrant suitable professional assessment.

c05s03 Chapter 5: Regulation involves connected systems.
Autonomic nervous system: regulation of many internal bodily functions. Sympathetic division: supports mobilization in many conditions. Parasympathetic division: supports regulation including recovery and digestion.

Autonomic pathways help regulate cardiovascular, digestive and other functions. Their effects are organ-specific and coordinated with central processes.

The common mobilize-and-recover description is useful but simplified. The divisions are not two personality types or exact opposites in every organ and situation.

c05s03b Chapter 5: Hormones signal through receptors.
Endocrine system: glands and signals regulating bodily functions. Feedback loop: system output influences subsequent regulation.

Hormones reach target tissues through circulation. Effects depend on receptors, timing and the state of the system. Feedback helps regulate activity over time.

A single hormone measurement can vary with time of day and sampling conditions. It is not a direct scale of personality, stress damage or moral character.

c05s04 Chapter 5: Arousal does not name the feeling.
Physiological arousal: change in bodily activation. Interoception: sensing internal bodily states. Appraisal: evaluation of a situation’s significance.

A fast heartbeat can accompany exertion, excitement, fear or other conditions. Interpretation also uses the situation, expectations and subjective experience.

Do not infer another person’s emotion from one bodily cue. Ask what they report and consider other explanations; persistent or concerning symptoms need appropriate medical advice.

c05s05 Chapter 5: What does the evidence allow?
Autonomic and endocrine measures help investigate regulation when sampling, context and suitable comparisons are specified. Individual signals usually lack psychological specificity.

A course explanation cannot diagnose a symptom or justify changing medication.

c06s03 Chapter 6: Acute responses can support action.
Stressor: a demand or threat capable of eliciting a response. Stress response: coordinated response to a perceived demand. Acute stress: a response over a relatively short period.

Stress responses can mobilize attention and resources for immediate demands. Whether a response helps depends on the task, its intensity and the available resources.

A useful short-term response is not evidence that prolonged strain is harmless. Likewise, a long-term risk association does not mean every alarm response causes damage.

c06s03b Chapter 6: Regulation has more than one pathway.
Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis: linked hormonal stress-regulation pathway. Allostasis: stability supported through changing regulation.

Stress regulation involves autonomic and hormonal systems, including the hypothalamic, pituitary and adrenal pathway. Cortisol has ordinary regulatory functions as well as changing with some stressors.

Its level depends on time, health and measurement conditions. A single sample cannot reveal a person’s complete stress history.

c06s04 Chapter 6: Repeated strain has social conditions.
Allostatic load: cumulative burden associated with repeated adaptation. Recovery: return or adjustment following demands. Confounding: other factors contributing to an association.

Research relates prolonged adversity and physiological regulation to health, using multiple measures and long timescales. Causal interpretation must consider health, resources and other exposures.

Workload, safety and material support matter. An individual relaxation exercise is not an adequate answer to every structural stressor.

c06s05 Chapter 6: What does the evidence allow?
Allostatic-load measures are composites, and studies differ in which indicators they include. Longitudinal associations do not turn one cortisol result into proof of damage.

Persistent distress or physical concerns need appropriate care. The course does not prescribe a biological treatment.

c07s03 Chapter 7: Two processes shape sleep timing.
Circadian rhythm: approximately daily biological cycle. Sleep pressure: need for sleep increasing with sustained wakefulness. Entrainment: alignment of a rhythm to external timing cues.

Sleep timing reflects both accumulated wakefulness and circadian regulation. Light is an important timing cue, alongside behaviour and social schedules.

Feeling alert does not necessarily mean a person is fully rested. Shift schedules can place biological timing and required activity in conflict.

c07s03b Chapter 7: Sleep is not one uniform state.
Non-rapid-eye-movement sleep: sleep stages without rapid eye movements. Rapid-eye-movement sleep: sleep stage with characteristic eye movements. Sleep architecture: organization of sleep stages across time.

Sleep cycles through stages identified using physiological measures. Stages differ in features such as brain activity, muscle tone and eye movements.

Sleep supports several functions, including aspects of memory and regulation. Dreams are not restricted to one simple function, and a stage label is not a unique psychological meaning.

c07s04 Chapter 7: A tracker is an estimate.

Consumer devices infer sleep from limited signals; their estimates are not equivalent to a complete clinical sleep study. Judge a device against validation for its claimed use.

Persistent sleep difficulties, breathing concerns or excessive sleepiness warrant appropriate care. Do not alter medication or undertake sleep deprivation as course practice.

c07s05 Chapter 7: What does the evidence allow?
Sleep findings depend on duration, timing, task and measurement. Laboratory deprivation studies inform specific outcomes without establishing every real-world effect.

Sleep needs and clinical problems vary. The course provides concepts rather than individualized sleep treatment.

c09s03 Chapter 9: Plasticity is change, not a promise.
Neuroplasticity: change in neural organization or function. Experience-dependent change: change associated with particular experience. Synaptic plasticity: change in the effectiveness of connections.

Nervous systems change with development, experience, injury and learning. Plasticity includes several mechanisms and timescales.

Calling an activity plasticity does not tell us whether a change is beneficial or durable. The effect must be measured against a specific outcome.

c09s03b Chapter 9: Practice can improve a specific task.
Near transfer: benefit on a closely related task. Far transfer: benefit on a substantially different task. Active control: comparison with a credible alternative activity.

Sam may learn the regularities of familiar repairs. That is useful even if general memory does not change.

To test a broader benefit, compare suitable groups on untrained outcomes, account for expectations and assess persistence. A pre-to-post improvement without a comparison leaves practice and other explanations open.

c09s04 Chapter 9: Rehabilitation is individualized.
Functional outcome: performance relevant to daily life. Prognosis: anticipated course with uncertainty.

Recovery and rehabilitation depend on the injury, health, time, goals and available care. Neural changes may support recovery without restoring every previous function.

Do not promise cures from a general plasticity claim. Professional plans should connect meaningful outcomes with suitable evidence and the person’s priorities.

c09s05 Chapter 9: What does the evidence allow?
Plasticity research shows that neural systems can change, but evidence for a particular training product requires its own controlled studies and meaningful outcomes.

Changes in an image or trained score do not automatically show durable far transfer or clinical recovery.

c10s03 Chapter 10: Connect levels without skipping steps.
Reductionism: explaining a complex phenomenon only at a narrower level. Multilevel explanation: linking relevant levels of analysis. Causal chain: specified sequence of influences.

A biological explanation can connect cells, circuits, behaviour and environment. Each link needs evidence rather than an attractive metaphor.

A brain correlate does not identify the only cause, and a social context is not biologically irrelevant. Ask how the proposed levels interact in the actual task.

c10s03b Chapter 10: Evaluate the intervention claim separately.
Generalizability: applicability to other settings or populations. Adverse effect: an unwanted consequence of an intervention.

A product can use accurate neuroscience terms while making an untested practical claim. Check the comparison group, sample, outcome, follow-up and unwanted effects.

Ask whether the benefit matters in everyday life and whether the study tested the actual product. Biological plausibility is not a substitute for efficacy evidence.

c10s04 Chapter 10: Keep choices proportionate.
Informed consent: agreement based on relevant information. Biological determinism: treating biology as a fixed inevitable cause. Uncertainty: limits in what evidence establishes.

Use the evidence to ask better questions and improve accessible conditions. Avoid diagnosing, prescribing or interpreting another person’s private biological data.

Health decisions need qualified care and informed choice. If a claim exceeds the evidence, narrow it rather than replacing it with another certainty.

c10s05 Chapter 10: What does the evidence allow?
A strong biological account uses suitable measurements, competing explanations and converging methods. Translation to individual care or products requires additional evidence.

This course is not medical training. Completing it does not establish competence to interpret scans, genetic tests or treatment choices.
